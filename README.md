This folder contains my Sprint Review and Retrospective for the SNHU Travel project. The document covers how I applied the roles, user stories, interruptions, communication practices, and organizational tools from the course, and it evaluates whether Scrum-Agile was the right approach for this project.

## Reflection Questions

### How do I interpret user needs and implement them into a program? How does creating "user stories" help with this?

I interpret user needs by turning them into specific, testable statements, and user stories are the tool that made this possible on SNHU Travel. A story such as "top five destination list" describes who needs something and why, and its acceptance criteria say what done looks like. The top five destination story worked well because its criteria matched its first test case closely, so there was a clear path from need to implementation to verification.

The vacation type and price filter stories showed the limits of this approach. The vacation type story lists six experience types, but the tests covered only three, and the requirement to keep filters in place during a session was never tested. I learned that a story is only as useful as the criteria behind it, and criteria only protect users when the tests cover all of them. Before testing, I also wrote clarification questions to the Product Owner, such as the minimum and maximum budget and how negative or text input should behave. Organizing those questions by story let the Product Owner answer quickly, and it meant I did not have to guess what users needed.

### How do I approach developing programs? What Agile processes do I hope to incorporate into my future development work?

I approach development in small, iterative steps. I want to prioritize a backlog, deliver working increments in short sprints, and check the work against clear criteria before calling it finished. Testing should start early, not at the end. In Module Six I proposed pair programming and code reviews so problems surface sooner and knowledge is shared across the team.

Some of the Agile processes I hope to carry into future work are:

- **Short sprints with planned refactoring.** The team agreed that each sprint should include time for technical debt, so cleanup does not get pushed aside by new features.
- **Testers involved from the beginning.** Continuous testing catches problems earlier than testing everything at the end.
- **Prioritized backlogs and clear acceptance criteria.** These kept our work organized, and the wellness travel change was manageable because of them.
- **Regular retrospectives.** The retrospective showed me gaps I would have missed, such as the untested acceptance criteria. I want to keep examining how well my process and tools are working.

I also learned that Scrum expects change, and I want my development process to absorb change without starting over. Scrum helped with the wellness change and the wireframe change. Waterfall could have worked for fixed requirements, but it would have forced rework once the requirements changed.

### What does it mean to be a good team member in software development?

A good team member keeps communication open and makes it easy for others to participate. In my Module Six posts, I started my replies with what I agreed with, so Dylan knew his point had landed, and then asked specific questions so he could explain his idea. When Dylan raised a concern about refactoring, I agreed with him and connected his concern about quality to my concern about technical debt. That showed we wanted the same outcome.

A good team member also protects the work and the people doing it. Christy, our Product Owner, kept the deadlines, moved other stories down the backlog, and asked the team what could realistically be finished. As the developer and Scrum Master for the retrospective, I want to be the person who keeps the process focused and who names gaps honestly, including gaps in my own work. Good teammates ask questions before guessing, follow up on commitments, and say clearly what was and was not tested.
